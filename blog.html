Old vs New Tax Regime 2026: Which One Saves You More?

By Raja Mahakul  ·  Published: June 10, 2026  ·  6 min read

1. Introduction

Every salaried Indian faces the same question at the start of every financial year: Old Tax Regime or New Tax Regime? With the Union Budget 2025-26 making the New Regime even more attractive — raising the basic exemption limit to ₹4 lakh and restructuring slabs — the choice has never been more important. This guide breaks down both regimes, compares them side-by-side, and helps you decide which one puts more money in your pocket for FY 2025-26 (AY 2026-27).

2. Old Tax Regime

The Old Tax Regime has been the default system for Indian taxpayers for decades. Its key feature is a rich set of deductions and exemptions that can significantly reduce your taxable income if you invest and plan well.

Key deductions available:

  • Section 80C – Up to ₹1.5 lakh (PPF, ELSS, LIC, home loan principal, etc.)
  • Section 80D – Health insurance premium up to ₹25,000 (₹50,000 for senior citizens)
  • HRA Exemption – House Rent Allowance for salaried employees in rented accommodation
  • Section 24(b) – Home loan interest up to ₹2 lakh
  • Standard Deduction – ₹50,000 for salaried individuals
  • NPS (80CCD) – Additional ₹50,000 over the 80C limit

Old Regime slabs (FY 2025-26): 0% up to ₹2.5L · 5% (₹2.5L–₹5L) · 20% (₹5L–₹10L) · 30% above ₹10L. A rebate under Section 87A makes income up to ₹5L effectively tax-free.

3. New Tax Regime (Default from FY 2024-25)

Introduced in Budget 2020 and made the default regime from FY 2023-24, the New Tax Regime offers lower slab rates in exchange for forgoing most deductions and exemptions.

Revised slabs for FY 2025-26:

  • Up to ₹4,00,000 – Nil
  • ₹4,00,001 – ₹8,00,000 – 5%
  • ₹8,00,001 – ₹12,00,000 – 10%
  • ₹12,00,001 – ₹16,00,000 – 15%
  • ₹16,00,001 – ₹20,00,000 – 20%
  • ₹20,00,001 – ₹24,00,000 – 25%
  • Above ₹24,00,000 – 30%

Under the New Regime, income up to ₹12 lakh is effectively tax-free for resident individuals (after the enhanced Section 87A rebate of ₹60,000). The standard deduction of ₹75,000 is also available, making the effective zero-tax threshold ₹12.75 lakh for salaried taxpayers.

5. Which One is Better?

The answer depends on your total deductions. Here is a practical rule of thumb for FY 2025-26:

  • Income up to ₹12.75 lakh? → New Regime is almost always better (zero tax after rebate + standard deduction).
  • Income ₹12.75L–₹18L with large deductions? → Run both calculations. Old Regime can win if 80C + HRA + 80D together exceed ₹4–5 lakh.
  • Income above ₹18L with heavy investments? → Old Regime often saves more if deductions exceed ₹5.5L+.
  • No investments or renting without HRA? → New Regime will almost always be cheaper.

6. Frequently Asked Questions

Can I switch regimes every year?

Salaried individuals (without business income) can switch between regimes every financial year. Those with business income can switch only once — from New to Old — and cannot switch back.

Is NPS deduction available in the New Regime?

The employer's contribution to NPS (Section 80CCD(2)) is still available as a deduction in the New Regime. The employee's own contribution under 80CCD(1B) is not.

What happens if I don't choose a regime?

From FY 2023-24 onward, the New Tax Regime is the default. If you do not submit Form 10-IEA, your employer will deduct TDS under the New Regime automatically.

Which regime is better for a ₹10 lakh salary?

For a ₹10 lakh gross salary, the New Regime typically results in zero or very low tax after the ₹60,000 rebate and ₹75,000 standard deduction. The Old Regime may save slightly more only if you have ₹1.5L in 80C + significant HRA + 80D deductions.

7. Conclusion

The New Tax Regime 2026 is a strong default choice for most Indian salaried employees — especially those earning up to ₹12.75 lakh or those with limited deductions. For higher earners with substantial investments, HRA, and home loans, the Old Regime may still offer meaningful tax savings.

CTC vs In-Hand Salary: What's the Difference?

By Raja Mahakul  ·  Published: June 11, 2026  ·  5 min read

1. Introduction

You just got a job offer. The letter says your CTC is ₹8,00,000 per year. You do the quick math — that's about ₹66,667 a month. But when your first salary actually hits your bank account, it's closer to ₹52,000. What happened to the rest?

This is one of the most common surprises for first-time employees in India. The gap between your CTC (Cost to Company) and your in-hand salary is real, and it's not a mistake. This guide explains exactly why, in plain language.

2. What is CTC?

CTC stands for Cost to Company. It is the total amount your employer spends on you in a year. This includes your actual salary plus all the extra costs the company pays on your behalf — like their share of your Provident Fund, gratuity provision, health insurance, and any other benefits.

Think of CTC as the employer's total bill for hiring you. It is not the amount you take home.

CTC typically includes:

  • Basic Salary – The fixed base component, usually 40–50% of CTC.
  • House Rent Allowance (HRA) – Paid to help cover rent costs.
  • Special / Performance Allowances – Variable top-up components.
  • Employer's PF Contribution – 12% of basic salary paid by your employer to your EPF account.
  • Gratuity – A retirement benefit provisioned at ~4.81% of basic salary per year.
  • Medical / Insurance Benefits – Any health cover the company provides.
  • Bonuses – Annual or performance-linked pay-outs (sometimes included, sometimes extra).

3. What is In-Hand (Take-Home) Salary?

In-hand salary (also called take-home salary) is the amount actually credited to your bank account every month after all deductions. It is always lower than what your CTC suggests, because several amounts are either deducted from your pay or set aside before you see the money.

4. Why Are CTC and In-Hand Salary Different?

The difference comes from two directions:

  • Items included in CTC but not paid as cash — For example, the employer's PF contribution and gratuity go into a retirement fund, not your bank account. Medical insurance is paid to a provider, not to you.
  • Deductions from your gross salary — Your employee PF contribution, Professional Tax, and TDS (income tax) are cut from your gross pay before you receive it.

5. Common Deductions Explained

Employee Provident Fund (EPF)

You contribute 12% of your Basic Salary every month to your EPF account. Your employer also contributes 12% — but that is already counted inside your CTC, so it does not increase your take-home. Your own 12% is deducted from your gross salary.

Professional Tax

Professional Tax is a state-level tax levied on salaried employees. The amount varies by state — in most states it is around ₹200 per month (₹2,400 per year), with the maximum capped at ₹2,500 per year. Not all states charge it (e.g., Delhi does not).

Income Tax (TDS)

Your employer deducts Tax Deducted at Source (TDS) from your salary every month based on your projected annual income and tax regime. If you declare investments under 80C, HRA, or other deductions to your employer, your TDS will be lower. If you do not declare anything, TDS will be higher. 7. Quick Tips to Maximise Your In-Hand Salary

  • Declare your HRA – If you pay rent, submit your rent receipts to your employer to reduce your TDS.
  • Use Section 80C – Declare PPF, ELSS, or LIC investments up to ₹1.5L to lower taxable income (Old Regime).
  • Choose the right tax regime – Use our Income Tax Calculator to compare Old vs New Regime and pick the one with lower TDS.
  • Check your salary structure – Ask HR if allowances can be restructured (e.g., meal coupons, LTA, internet allowance) — some are fully or partially tax-exempt.
  • Voluntary PF opt-out – For salaries above ₹15,000 basic, you may choose not to contribute to PF beyond the mandatory limit, increasing take-home.

8. Conclusion

CTC is your employer's total cost — it includes components that never land in your account. In-hand salary is what you actually get after PF contributions, Professional Tax, and income tax deductions. For a ₹8 LPA CTC, you can realistically expect around ₹50,000–₹54,000 in hand per month.

EMI Calculator Guide: How to Calculate Your Loan EMI in 2026

By Raja Mahakul  ·  Published: June 12, 2026  ·  6 min read

1. What Is an EMI and Why Does It Matter?

An EMI (Equated Monthly Instalment) is the fixed amount you pay every month towards repaying a loan — whether it's a home loan, car loan, personal loan, or education loan. Each EMI includes a portion of the principal (the amount you borrowed) and a portion of the interest charged by the lender.

Knowing your EMI in advance helps you plan your monthly budget, compare loan offers from different banks, and decide on the right loan amount and tenure before you sign on the dotted line. That's exactly what Numvexa's free EMI Calculator helps you do — instantly, with no sign-up required.

2. The EMI Formula Explained

EMI is calculated using a standard mathematical formula based on the loan amount, interest rate, and tenure:

EMI = [P × R × (1+R)N] / [(1+R)N − 1]
  • P = Principal loan amount
  • R = Monthly interest rate (annual rate ÷ 12 ÷ 100)
  • N = Loan tenure in months

Doing this calculation by hand every time you compare loan offers is tedious and error-prone. Numvexa's EMI Calculator applies this formula instantly — just enter your loan amount, interest rate, and tenure to see your EMI, total interest payable, and total repayment amount.

3. Factors That Affect Your EMI Amount

Loan Amount (Principal)

The higher the amount you borrow, the higher your EMI — assuming the interest rate and tenure stay the same. Borrowing ₹10 lakh instead of ₹5 lakh roughly doubles your EMI.

Interest Rate

Even a small change in interest rate has a big impact over a long tenure. A 1% higher rate on a 20-year home loan can add lakhs of rupees in total interest paid over the loan's life.

Loan Tenure

A longer tenure reduces your monthly EMI but increases the total interest you pay over the life of the loan. A shorter tenure means higher EMIs but significant interest savings overall.

5. Tips to Reduce Your EMI Burden

  • Make a larger down payment – Reducing your principal lowers both your EMI and total interest.
  • Choose a shorter tenure if affordable – You pay more per month but save significantly on total interest.
  • Compare interest rates across lenders – Even a 0.25–0.5% difference adds up over a long tenure.
  • Make prepayments when possible – Any lump-sum prepayment towards the principal reduces future interest.
  • Maintain a good credit score – A higher score often qualifies you for better interest rates.

6. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

Q1. Does EMI remain the same throughout the loan tenure?

For fixed-rate loans, yes — the EMI amount stays constant. However, the proportion of principal and interest within each EMI changes over time: early EMIs have a higher interest component, while later EMIs are mostly principal.

Q2. What happens if I miss an EMI payment?

Missing an EMI typically attracts a late payment penalty and can negatively affect your credit score. Repeated defaults may lead to legal action by the lender, especially for secured loans like home or car loans.

Q3. Can I change my EMI amount after the loan is sanctioned?

Some lenders allow you to restructure your loan — increasing or decreasing the tenure to adjust the EMI — but this usually involves processing fees and requires lender approval.

Q4. Is a longer tenure always a bad idea?

Not necessarily. A longer tenure can make EMIs more affordable and easier to manage within your monthly budget, but it increases the total interest paid. The right choice depends on your cash flow and financial goals.

An EMI calculator tells you the monthly instalment for a given loan amount, rate, and tenure. A loan eligibility calculator estimates the maximum amount you can borrow based on your income. Numvexa's EMI Calculator focuses on the former — helping you plan repayments for a known loan amount.

7. Conclusion

Understanding how your EMI is calculated puts you in control of your loan decisions — whether you're buying a home, a car, or financing an education. Before you commit to a loan, always run the numbers through an EMI calculator to compare different loan amounts, interest rates, and tenures.

SIP Calculator Guide: How to Plan Your Mutual Fund Investments

By Raja Mahakul  ·  Published: June 12, 2026  ·  6 min read

1. What Is a SIP?

A SIP (Systematic Investment Plan) is a method of investing a fixed amount of money in a mutual fund at regular intervals — usually monthly — instead of investing a lump sum all at once. SIPs are one of the most popular ways for Indians to build long-term wealth, because they instil financial discipline and let you benefit from rupee cost averaging and compounding.

If you've ever wondered how much your monthly SIP of ₹5,000 will grow to in 15 or 20 years, Numvexa's free SIP Calculator gives you the answer in seconds.

2. The SIP Formula Explained

The future value of a SIP investment is calculated using the formula:

M = P × [(1+i)n − 1] / i × (1+i)
  • M = Maturity amount (what you receive at the end)
  • P = Amount invested at regular intervals (monthly instalment)
  • i = Monthly rate of return (annual expected return ÷ 12 ÷ 100)
  • n = Total number of instalments (months)

This formula assumes your returns compound monthly. In reality, mutual fund returns fluctuate, so the calculator's result is an estimate based on a constant assumed rate of return — useful for planning, not a guarantee.

3. The Power of Compounding

Compounding means your returns start earning their own returns. The longer your investment horizon, the more dramatic the effect — which is why starting early matters more than investing large amounts later.

5. What Is a Step-Up SIP?

A step-up SIP (or top-up SIP) lets you automatically increase your monthly investment amount by a fixed percentage every year — typically matching your expected salary increments. This helps you invest more as your income grows, without feeling the pinch, and can significantly boost your final corpus compared to a flat SIP.

6. Tips for Successful SIP Investing

  • Start early – Even a small monthly amount grows substantially over 15-20 years due to compounding.
  • Stay invested through market dips – SIPs work best when you continue investing during downturns, buying more units at lower prices.
  • Increase your SIP periodically – Use a step-up approach to align investments with your rising income.
  • Diversify across fund categories – Combine large-cap, mid-cap, and debt funds based on your risk appetite and goals.
  • Review annually, not daily – Frequent checking can lead to panic decisions; review your portfolio once or twice a year.

7. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

Q1. Is the SIP Calculator's result guaranteed?

No. The calculator estimates returns based on a constant rate of return that you input. Actual mutual fund returns fluctuate with market performance, so the maturity value is an illustrative projection, not a promise.

Q2. What is a good expected return rate to use?

Many investors use 10-12% as a long-term estimate for diversified equity mutual funds in India, based on historical averages. However, returns vary by fund category and market conditions, so it's wise to test multiple scenarios (e.g., 8%, 10%, 12%) in the calculator.

Q3. Can I withdraw my SIP investment anytime?

Most open-ended mutual funds allow withdrawal anytime, though some may charge an exit load if redeemed within a specified period (often 1 year). ELSS (tax-saving) funds have a mandatory 3-year lock-in.

Q4. SIP vs lump sum — which is better?

SIPs reduce the risk of investing a large amount at a market peak by spreading purchases over time (rupee cost averaging). Lump sum investing can outperform in consistently rising markets, but SIPs are generally easier for salaried individuals to commit to consistently.

Q5. How does SIP relate to loan repayment planning?

If you're repaying a loan EMI while also investing via SIP, it helps to plan both together so neither strains your monthly budget. Use Numvexa's EMI Calculator alongside the SIP Calculator to balance debt repayment and wealth creation.

8. Conclusion

SIPs make investing accessible and disciplined — you don't need a large lump sum to start building wealth, just consistency over time. The earlier you start and the longer you stay invested, the more powerful compounding becomes.

GST Calculator Guide: How to Calculate GST Amount Easily

By Raja Mahakul  ·  Published: June 12, 2026  ·  6 min read

1. What Is GST and Why You Need a GST Calculator?

GST (Goods and Services Tax) is a single, unified indirect tax applied on the supply of goods and services across India. It replaced multiple older taxes like VAT, service tax, and excise duty. Whether you're a business owner pricing a product, a freelancer raising an invoice, or a shopper trying to figure out how much tax you're paying — calculating GST correctly matters.

Manually adding or removing GST from an amount can be confusing, especially when prices are quoted "inclusive of GST." That's where Numvexa's free GST Calculator helps — instantly compute the GST amount, the base price, and the final price in either direction.

2. GST Formula Explained

Adding GST (GST Exclusive → Inclusive)

GST Amount = (Original Price × GST Rate) / 100
Final Price = Original Price + GST Amount

Removing GST (GST Inclusive → Exclusive)

Original Price = Final Price × 100 / (100 + GST Rate)
GST Amount = Final Price − Original Price

India currently has GST slabs of 0%, 5%, 12%, 18%, and 28% depending on the category of goods or services.

3. CGST, SGST, and IGST — What's the Difference?

For transactions within the same state, GST is split equally between Central GST (CGST) and State GST (SGST) — each being half the total GST rate. For example, on an 18% GST item, you'd see 9% CGST + 9% SGST on the invoice.

For inter-state transactions, the entire amount is charged as Integrated GST (IGST), equal to the total GST rate, and is collected by the central government and apportioned to the destination state.

5. Common GST Calculation Mistakes

  • Confusing inclusive and exclusive prices – Always check whether the quoted price already includes GST before applying the formula.
  • Applying the wrong GST slab – Different goods and services fall under different rates (5%, 12%, 18%, 28%); verify the correct rate for your item.
  • Forgetting CGST/SGST split on invoices – For intra-state sales, the GST must be shown as two equal components, not as a single line.
  • Rounding errors on large invoices – Small rounding differences can add up across many line items; use a calculator for accuracy.

6. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

Q1. What are the current GST slabs in India?

The common GST slabs are 0%, 5%, 12%, 18%, and 28%, with some special rates for specific items like gold and precious stones. The applicable rate depends on the HSN/SAC classification of the goods or service.

Q2. How do I know if a price is GST-inclusive or exclusive?

Check the invoice or price tag — it should explicitly state "inclusive of GST" or "+ GST extra." If unspecified, ask the seller, as the calculation differs significantly between the two.

Q3. Can I use this calculator for both CGST+SGST and IGST?

Yes. The total GST amount is the same whether it's split as CGST+SGST (intra-state) or charged fully as IGST (inter-state) — only the invoice presentation differs, not the total tax calculated.

Q4. Does GST apply to all goods and services?

No. Certain items like fresh fruits, vegetables, and unprocessed food are exempt (0% GST), while items like alcohol and petroleum products fall outside the GST regime and are taxed separately by states.

Q5. How does GST affect my monthly budgeting alongside EMIs?

GST adds to the cost of goods and services you purchase, which affects your overall monthly expenses. If you're also repaying a loan, use Numvexa's EMI Calculator alongside the GST Calculator to get a complete picture of your monthly outflow.

7. Conclusion

Understanding GST calculations helps you price products correctly, verify invoices, and avoid overpaying or underpaying tax. Whether you need to add GST to a base price or extract the base price from a GST-inclusive amount, the formula is straightforward once you know which direction to apply it.

Age Calculator Guide: Calculate Your Exact Age in Years, Months & Days

By Raja Mahakul  ·  Published: June 12, 2026  ·  5 min read

1. Why Calculate Your Exact Age?

2. How Is Age Calculated?

Age calculation involves finding the difference between your date of birth and a reference date (usually today), broken down into years, months, and days. The general approach:

  • Subtract the birth year from the current year to get a base number of years.
  • If the current month/day is before the birth month/day, subtract one year and adjust the months accordingly.

  • Account for leap years (every 4 years, with exceptions for century years not divisible by 400) since February has 29 days in those years.

This is why a simple subtraction of years often gives an incorrect result — the exact months and days component requires careful date arithmetic.

Percentage Calculator Guide: Quick Formulas for Everyday Calculations

1. Why Percentages Matter in Everyday Life

Percentages are everywhere — discounts at a store, GST on your bill, interest rates on loans, salary hikes, exam scores, and investment returns. A percentage simply expresses a number as a fraction of 100, making it easy to compare proportions across different contexts.

While the math behind percentages is simple, it's easy to make small errors — especially when calculating percentage increase versus percentage of a number, or working backwards from a final value. This guide covers the core formulas with practical examples relevant to Indian financial life.

2. Core Percentage Formulas

Finding a Percentage of a Number

Result = (Percentage × Number) / 100

Example: What is 18% of ₹2,000? → (18 × 2000) / 100 = ₹360

Percentage Increase / Decrease

% Change = [(New Value − Old Value) / Old Value] × 100

Example: Your salary increased from ₹50,000 to ₹56,000. % increase = [(56000−50000)/50000] × 100 = 12%

Finding the Original Value (Reverse Percentage)

Original Value = Final Value / (1 ± Percentage/100)

Example: A product costs ₹1,180 after adding 18% GST. Original price = 1180 / 1.18 = ₹1,000.

4. Tips to Avoid Common Percentage Mistakes

  • Always identify the "base" value – percentages are meaningless without knowing what they're a percentage of.
  • Don't confuse percentage points with percentages – a rate going from 10% to 12% is a 2 percentage-point increase, but a 20% relative increase.
  • For successive percentage changes, don't simply add them – a 10% increase followed by a 10% decrease does not bring you back to the original value.
  • When reversing GST or discounts, divide by (1 + rate) or (1 − rate), don't just subtract the percentage from the total.

5. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

Q1. How do I calculate what percentage one number is of another?

Divide the part by the whole and multiply by 100: (Part / Whole) × 100. For example, 45 out of 180 is (45/180) × 100 = 25%.

Q2. Why doesn't a 10% increase followed by a 10% decrease return to the original value?

Because the second percentage is calculated on a different (larger or smaller) base. ₹100 increased by 10% becomes ₹110; decreasing ₹110 by 10% gives ₹99, not ₹100.

Q3. How is percentage used in GST calculations?

GST is a percentage of the base price added on top (for exclusive pricing) or embedded within the final price (for inclusive pricing). See our GST Calculator Guide for detailed formulas.

Use the percentage change formula: [(Current Value − Invested Amount) / Invested Amount] × 100. This gives your overall return percentage on a SIP investment, which you can also estimate in advance using our SIP Calculator.

Q5. Can percentages be greater than 100%?

Yes. A percentage greater than 100% simply means the value exceeds the base it's being compared to — for example, if an investment doubles, that's a 100% increase, and if it triples, that's a 200% increase.

Percentages form the backbone of everyday financial decisions — from understanding discounts and taxes to tracking salary growth and investment returns. Mastering these few formulas helps you quickly verify bills, compare offers, and make informed money decisions.

Many employees withdraw their EPF when they resign, treating it as a windfall rather than a retirement fund. A premature withdrawal of ₹5 lakh could cost you ₹50,000–₹75,000 in taxes alone.

Incorrect Aadhaar, PAN, or bank details linked to your UAN (Universal Account Number) can cause delayed or failed settlements at the time of withdrawal or transfer. KYC updates are easy to complete on the EPFO member portal — and should be done immediately when you join a new employer.

5. Ignoring the Voluntary Provident Fund (VPF) Option

Many salaried employees are unaware that they can voluntarily contribute more than the mandatory 12% of their basic salary to their PF account. These additional contributions earn the same 8.25% interest, are eligible for Section 80C deduction up to ₹1.5 lakh, and the interest is tax-free. VPF is one of the safest and most tax-efficient savings vehicles available — yet it is almost never used.

7. Benefits of Long-Term Investing

  • Compounding works in your favour – The longer your money stays invested, the larger the proportion of your final corpus that comes from returns rather than your own contributions.
  • Rupee cost averaging – By investing the same amount every month, you automatically buy more units when prices are low and fewer when prices are high, smoothing out market volatility.

8. Common SIP Mistakes to Avoid

  • Ignoring asset allocation – Putting all your SIPs into high-risk equity funds without any debt allocation can be risky closer to your goal date.
  • Withdrawing early for non-essential expenses – Breaking a long-term SIP for short-term wants defeats the purpose of compounding.

Best Budget Rule (50/30/20) for Salaried Employees

By Raja Mahakul  ·  Published: June 13, 2026  ·  8 min read

1. What is the 50/30/20 Rule?

The 50/30/20 rule is a simple budgeting framework that divides your take-home (in-hand) salary into three buckets:

  • 50% for Needs – Rent, groceries, utility bills, EMIs, insurance premiums, and other essential expenses.
  • 30% for Wants – Dining out, entertainment, shopping, travel, subscriptions, and lifestyle upgrades.
  • 20% for Savings & Investments – Emergency fund, SIPs, retirement savings, and debt repayment beyond minimums.

This rule became popular because it is easy to remember and apply, without needing detailed expense tracking for every rupee. For salaried employees in India, where salary structures and costs of living vary widely across cities, the 50/30/20 rule offers a flexible starting point that can be adjusted to your situation.

2. Needs vs Wants vs Savings — How to Tell Them Apart

The hardest part of this rule is correctly classifying your expenses. Here's a simple way to think about it:

Needs (50%)

These are expenses you cannot avoid without affecting your basic living standard or financial obligations:

  • House rent or home loan EMI
  • Groceries and household essentials
  • Electricity, water, gas, and other utility bills
  • Health insurance and term insurance premiums
  • Minimum loan EMIs (car, personal loan, etc.)
  • Commute costs (fuel, public transport, cab to work)
  • School fees for children

Wants (30%)

These improve your quality of life but are not essential for survival or basic obligations:

  • Eating out, food delivery, and cafes
  • OTT subscriptions (Netflix, Prime, etc.)
  • Shopping for clothes, gadgets, and accessories beyond necessity
  • Vacations and weekend trips
  • Movies, hobbies, and entertainment
  • Upgrading your phone or car earlier than needed

Savings & Investments (20%)

This is money that builds your future financial security:

  • Emergency fund contributions
  • SIPs in mutual funds
  • PPF, EPF voluntary contributions, NPS
  • Extra EMI prepayments to close loans faster
  • Recurring deposits or fixed deposits for goals

4. Budget Planning for Individuals and Families

For individuals (especially in metro cities like Bengaluru, Mumbai, or Delhi): Rent alone can eat up 25-35% of your salary, which may push your "Needs" bucket beyond 50%. If this happens, consider trimming the "Wants" category temporarily, or look for a more affordable living arrangement (shared flat, slightly farther location with better connectivity).

For families with dependents: School fees, healthcare for parents, and household expenses for multiple people often push the "Needs" percentage higher — sometimes to 60% or more. In such cases, a modified 60/20/20 or 55/25/20 split may be more realistic, while still protecting the 20% savings rate as a non-negotiable minimum.

The key principle isn't the exact percentages — it's making sure savings happen first, ideally through automated transfers right after your salary is credited, so that spending adjusts to what's left rather than savings being an afterthought.

5. Why an Emergency Fund Matters in Your Budget

Before your 20% savings bucket goes entirely into SIPs or long-term investments, a portion should first build an emergency fund — typically 3 to 6 months of essential expenses kept in a liquid, easily accessible form like a savings account or liquid mutual fund.

An emergency fund protects you from job loss, medical emergencies, or unexpected major expenses without forcing you to break long-term investments early or take on high-interest debt. Once your emergency fund is fully built, you can redirect that portion of your 20% entirely toward SIPs, retirement accounts, and other long-term goals.

6. Common Budgeting Mistakes

  • Treating savings as optional – Spending first and "saving whatever is left" usually results in little to no savings. Automate savings instead.
  • Misclassifying wants as needs – Daily food delivery or premium OTT subscriptions often get mentally filed under "needs" when they're really "wants."
  • Not accounting for irregular expenses – Annual insurance premiums, festival shopping, or vehicle servicing should be budgeted monthly (divided by 12) so they don't derail your plan when they occur.
  • Ignoring lifestyle inflation – As salary increases, expenses (especially "wants") often increase proportionally or more, leaving the savings percentage unchanged or even reduced.
  • No buffer for credit card bills – Spending on credit cards without tracking can silently break the 50/30/20 split each month.
  • Setting unrealistic targets – If 50/30/20 doesn't fit your current situation, an extreme cutback rarely sticks. Start with a percentage you can realistically maintain and improve gradually.

7. Practical Implementation Guide

  1. Calculate your exact in-hand salary using Numvexa's Salary Calculator.
  2. List all your monthly "Needs" — rent, EMIs, utilities, groceries, insurance, school fees, commute.
  3. Compare your Needs total to 50% of in-hand salary. If it's higher, look for ways to reduce fixed costs (renegotiate rent, refinance loans, switch to a cheaper plan).
  4. Set up an automatic transfer for savings (20%) on salary day — to a separate savings account, recurring deposit, or SIP — before you can spend it.
  5. Use the remaining amount for "Wants" — and track it loosely using a simple notes app or budgeting app.
  6. Review every 3 months — adjust the percentages as your salary, rent, or family situation changes.
  7. Increase savings percentage with every raise — even a 2-3% increase in your savings rate compounds significantly over years.

Q1. Is the 50/30/20 rule based on gross salary or in-hand salary?

Q2. What if my rent alone is more than 50% of my salary?

This is common in expensive metro cities. In such cases, you may need a modified split (e.g., 60/20/20) temporarily, while still protecting at least some savings. Long-term, consider whether a different living arrangement could free up budget for savings.

Q3. Should EMIs be counted under Needs or Savings?

Loan EMIs (home loan, car loan, personal loan) are generally counted under Needs since they are contractual obligations. However, any extra prepayment beyond the required EMI can be counted under Savings, as it reduces future interest and builds equity.

The 50/30/20 rule isn't a rigid law — it's a simple, flexible starting framework that helps salaried Indians take control of their money without complicated spreadsheets. The most important takeaway is to make savings non-negotiable and automatic, rather than something you do only if money is left over.

How to Save Income Tax in India: 10 Legal Ways to Reduce Your Tax in 2026

By Raja Mahakul  ·  Published: June 13, 2026  ·  9 min read

Introduction

Every year, lakhs of Indian taxpayers end up paying more tax than necessary simply because they don't know — or don't use — the legal deductions and exemptions available to them. The good news is that the Income Tax Act offers many ways to legally reduce your taxable income, provided you plan ahead and choose the right tax regime.

In this guide, we cover 10 legal ways to save income tax in India for FY 2025-26 (Assessment Year 2026-27), along with how the Old vs New Tax Regime affects which of these benefits you can actually claim.

1. Old vs New Tax Regime: Choose Wisely

This is the single most important decision in your tax planning. The New Tax Regime offers lower slab rates but removes most deductions and exemptions. The Old Tax Regime has higher slab rates but allows deductions like 80C, HRA, home loan interest, and more.

  • If your total eligible deductions (80C + HRA + 80D + home loan interest, etc.) exceed roughly ₹4-5 lakh, the Old Regime may save you more tax.
  • If you have few investments, no home loan, and don't claim HRA, the New Regime is usually better due to its lower rates and higher rebate threshold.

The best approach is to calculate both using Numvexa's free Income Tax Calculator and pick whichever results in lower tax for your specific income and deductions.

2. HRA (House Rent Allowance) Exemption

If you live in rented accommodation and receive HRA as part of your salary, you can claim an exemption under Section 10(13A) — but only under the Old Regime. The exempt amount is the lowest of:

  • Actual HRA received from employer
  • Rent paid minus 10% of basic salary
  • 50% of basic salary (for metro cities) or 40% (for non-metro cities)

To calculate your exact HRA exemption based on your salary, rent, and city, use Numvexa's HRA Calculator.

3. Section 80C — Up to ₹1.5 Lakh Deduction

Section 80C is the most widely used deduction, allowing you to reduce taxable income by up to ₹1,50,000 per year (Old Regime only) through investments and expenses such as:

  • Employee Provident Fund (EPF) contributions
  • Public Provident Fund (PPF)
  • Equity Linked Savings Scheme (ELSS) mutual funds
  • Life insurance premiums
  • 5-year tax-saving fixed deposits
  • Principal repayment on home loan
  • Children's tuition fees (up to 2 children)
  • Sukanya Samriddhi Yojana

Since the ₹1.5 lakh limit is shared across all these options, it's wise to plan a combination that matches your goals — for example, EPF + PPF + ELSS together.

4. ELSS (Equity Linked Savings Scheme)

ELSS mutual funds are one of the most attractive 80C options because they combine tax saving with the potential for equity-linked growth. Key points:

  • Lock-in period of just 3 years — the shortest among all 80C options
  • Invested in equity markets, so returns have historically been higher than PPF or FDs over the long term (though subject to market risk)
  • Can be started via SIP, so you can invest monthly rather than as a lump sum
  • Gains are subject to capital gains tax on redemption

If you're considering ELSS via SIP, project your potential returns using Numvexa's SIP Calculator.

5. PPF (Public Provident Fund)

PPF is a government-backed, long-term savings scheme with a 15-year lock-in (extendable in blocks of 5 years). Contributions up to ₹1.5 lakh per year qualify for 80C deduction, and the interest earned along with maturity proceeds are completely tax-free — making PPF an "EEE" (Exempt-Exempt-Exempt) investment. It's ideal for risk-averse investors building a retirement corpus alongside other instruments.

6. EPF (Employees' Provident Fund)

If you're a salaried employee, a portion of your salary (typically 12% of basic + DA) is automatically deducted and contributed to your EPF account, with a matching employer contribution. Your own EPF contribution qualifies for the 80C deduction (within the overall ₹1.5 lakh limit). Many salaried employees don't realise that their EPF contribution alone often uses up a large chunk of their 80C limit — so factor this in before making additional 80C investments.

7. NPS (National Pension System)

NPS offers tax benefits beyond the regular 80C limit:

  • Section 80CCD(1B) – An additional deduction of up to ₹50,000 for self-contributions, over and above the ₹1.5 lakh 80C limit (Old Regime).
  • Section 80CCD(2) – Employer's contribution to NPS (up to 10-14% of basic salary depending on employer type) is deductible, and importantly, this benefit is available even under the New Regime.

NPS is a market-linked retirement product with a mix of equity and debt, and withdrawals are partially taxable at retirement, with mandatory annuitisation of a portion of the corpus.

8. Health Insurance Deductions (Section 80D)

Premiums paid for health insurance (Old Regime only) qualify for deduction under Section 80D:

  • Up to ₹25,000 for self, spouse, and children (under 60 years)
  • Up to ₹50,000 if you or your spouse is a senior citizen (60+ years)
  • An additional ₹25,000-₹50,000 for parents' health insurance, depending on their age
  • Preventive health check-ups (up to ₹5,000, within the overall limit)

This means a taxpayer with senior citizen parents could potentially claim up to ₹1,00,000 in total 80D deductions (₹25,000/₹50,000 for self + ₹50,000 for parents).

9. Home Loan Tax Benefits

If you have a home loan (Old Regime), you can claim two separate deductions:

  • Section 24(b) – Deduction of up to ₹2,00,000 per year on home loan interest for a self-occupied property.
  • Section 80C – Principal repayment of the home loan is included within the overall ₹1.5 lakh 80C limit.

For let-out (rented) properties, there's no upper cap on interest deduction under Section 24(b), though the overall loss from house property that can be set off against other income is capped at ₹2 lakh per year, with the balance carried forward.

10. Education Loan Benefits (Section 80E)

Interest paid on an education loan taken for higher education (for yourself, spouse, children, or a student for whom you are a legal guardian) is fully deductible under Section 80E — with no upper limit on the amount, for up to 8 years from the year repayment begins. This deduction is available only on the interest component, not the principal, and only under the Old Regime.

11. Common Tax Planning Mistakes

  • Last-minute, rushed investments – Making 80C investments in March without planning often leads to choosing unsuitable products just to save tax, not to build wealth.
  • Not comparing both tax regimes – Sticking with the same regime every year without recalculating can mean leaving money on the table.
  • Forgetting employer's EPF and NPS contributions – These count toward your deductions/exemptions and should be factored into your planning.
  • Ignoring HRA documentation – Not keeping rent receipts, rental agreements, or landlord PAN (for rent above ₹1 lakh/year) can lead to HRA exemption being disallowed.
  • Overlooking Section 80E and 80D for parents – Many taxpayers don't claim education loan interest or parents' health insurance simply because they forget these exist.
  • Choosing tax-saving products with long lock-ins unnecessarily – If liquidity matters, ELSS (3-year lock-in) may suit better than PPF (15-year) for part of your 80C allocation.

12. Frequently Asked Questions (FAQs)

Q1. Can I claim both HRA and home loan interest deduction?

Yes, in certain situations — for example, if you own a home in one city but live on rent in another city for work, you can claim HRA exemption for the rented house and home loan interest deduction (Section 24b) for the owned house, both under the Old Regime.

Q2. Which deductions are still available under the New Regime?

Under the New Regime, most deductions are removed, but a few remain — notably the standard deduction, employer's NPS contribution (80CCD(2)), and deduction for contributions to the Agniveer Corpus Fund. HRA, 80C, 80D, home loan interest, and 80E are generally not available under the New Regime.

Q3. How do I know which regime saves me more tax?

The only reliable way is to calculate your tax liability under both regimes using your actual income and deduction figures. Numvexa's Income Tax Calculator does this comparison instantly.

Q4. Is ELSS better than PPF for tax saving?

It depends on your risk appetite and time horizon. ELSS offers market-linked returns with a shorter 3-year lock-in but carries market risk. PPF offers guaranteed, tax-free returns with a longer 15-year lock-in. Many investors use a combination of both for balance.

Q5. Can I claim HRA exemption if I pay rent to my parents?

Yes, this is legally allowed, provided the arrangement is genuine — you actually pay rent (preferably via bank transfer), your parents declare this rental income in their tax return, and you have a proper rent agreement. The property should ideally be owned by the parent and not jointly by you.

13. Conclusion

Saving tax legally is about planning ahead, not scrambling in the last week of March. Start by deciding between the Old and New Tax Regime based on your actual numbers, then systematically use the deductions that fit your life — HRA if you're renting, 80C for long-term savings, 80D for health insurance, and home or education loan benefits if applicable.
